## Introduction

I've been one of the many people reporting incredible changes to how I work with LLMs after asking them to respond in ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English. Fighting the bots and all of their superfluous statements, strange word choices, and general opacity has made working with them feeling far less exciting than it once did, and I'm fascinated by how difficult it still feels to wrangle an enjoyable relationship to working with AI. Yes, their ability to write complex code and help me structure new technical efforts has increased *dramatically*, but I still *hate* using them most of the time. It's not a healthy work flow, and more often than not I just catch myself telling them to "continue", checking out of the task early just to then be frustrated later when I use the final result and realize that it made a number of terrible choices that were buried in overly verbose outputs with an impossible information density.  

The STE100 trick has helped significantly, but it really only works on narrow well-defined software tasks. Sometimes I catch myself using it for other tasks, like editing a piece of writing or helping create a plan for something non-technical, but that got me thinking...

How much do we know about how stylistic choices impact model performance, and importantly, at what cost (or benefit) to the user?

Essentially, I'm interested in the human-model relationship, and finding a set of optimal prompting patterns across tasks to improve that relationship as much as possible. 

I believe this goes beyond simple "performance" as narrowly defined by benchmark/eval scores. For example, I'm willing to take a minor hit to accuracy and performance for an increase in *momentum* and a style that keeps me engaged in the task because my clearer engagement often catches poor decisions much earlier, maintains my sense of agency, and promotes a much healthier relationship that actually improves my life as a whole.

I'm deciding to leave out "[Expertise Advantage](https://aclanthology.org/2025.emnlp-main.1364.pdf)" as a legitimately viable prompting strategy, at this point, I don't think we need to tell the AI "You are an expert in mathematics" to improve mathematical performance, and the evidence here is pretty simple... it solved (apparently) the [double cycle cover conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cycle_double_cover) using this [prompt](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/04d1d1e4-bc75-476a-97cf-49055cd98d31/cdc_prompt.pdf), which has no "You are a ... " framing. 

Expertise advantage just feels a bit outdated at this point.
